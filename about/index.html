How a call works

  1. You call. A dispatcher asks for your address, where the water came from and what it touched.
  2. You get first steps. That usually means shutting off the main valve, staying out of rooms where water meets electricity and lifting belongings off wet floors.
  3. Scope and price are explained. Before work starts, the crew describes what it will do and what drives the cost.
  4. A crew is sent. Same-day response is available when crews are free, and we never promise a specific arrival time.
  5. Work is documented. Photos, moisture readings and drying logs are kept so you can share them with your insurer.

Why the type of water matters

Restoration crews sort water into categories because the category decides how the job is done. Industry practice is published in the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for professional water damage restoration, and it describes clean, gray and contaminated water. A supply line leak starts as clean water, but it can turn worse if materials stay wet for days.

Water from a sewer backup or a creek is treated as contaminated from the start. In that case porous materials such as carpet pad and drywall are often removed instead of dried. Our flood damage restoration page explains the safety steps in more detail.

Questions worth asking any restoration company

  • Who answers the phone at night, and is it the same line that dispatches the crew?
  • Will you explain the scope and the price before work starts?
  • What readings will you take, and will I get a copy?
  • Which standard do your crews follow for drying and for mold?
  • What can you not promise, such as arrival time or insurance coverage?

What you can do before a crew arrives

The first hour matters most. Close the main valve if a pipe is the source, and move small items, electronics and paperwork off wet floors. Open cabinet doors under a leaking sink so air can reach the wall behind them, and take photos before you move furniture.

Keep people and pets out of any room where water touches outlets or the electrical panel. If you smell gas or see water near the panel, call the utility or 911 first. Then call our line, because the dispatcher can add steps that fit your situation.

Homes of every age across the area

Houses in North Richland Hills range from the 1950s subdivisions that grew out of the Cummings Drive area to far newer homes on the north side. Older houses tend to have aging supply lines and water heaters, and newer ones tend to have appliance hose and condensate line failures that nobody notices for days.

Neither age is safer. What matters is how quickly the water is found and dried, which is why our line is staffed every hour of every day. Ask your insurer how your policy treats sudden leaks, slow leaks, sewer backups and flood water, since those four are handled differently.

If you are weighing repairs, ask the city building department whether your project needs a permit. North Richland Hills runs an online permits portal, and the answer depends on the work, not on us. We can describe what we found and what needs rebuilding, and the city decides what it requires.
