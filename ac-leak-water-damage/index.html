How an air conditioner ends up leaking into the house

A cooling system pulls moisture out of the air as it works, and that water has to leave through a condensate drain line. When algae and debris clog the line, the water backs up into the drain pan under the coil. When the pan fills, it spills onto whatever sits below.

Many air handlers sit in an attic or a closet, directly over finished ceilings and walls. A secondary pan under the unit is meant to catch overflow, but it can fill too. Because the water arrives a little at a time, the damage tends to build quietly in insulation and drywall.

Signs that the water is coming from the AC and not the roof

A ceiling stain below an attic unit is the classic sign. The better clue is timing. A stain that grows only while the system is running points at the condensate line, because the unit produces water only when it cools. A stain that grows after a rainstorm and holds still on dry days points up at the roof, and our roof leak water damage page covers that path.

  • A brown ring that spreads on hot afternoons and stalls overnight.
  • Water in the secondary pan or a wet closet floor around the air handler.
  • A musty smell near a return vent or the closet door.
  • Damp baseboard or carpet under a wall that holds the unit.

If you cannot tell, shut the system off for a day and watch the stain. A technician can confirm the source.

Safety in the first hour of an AC leak

Water near an air handler means water near electrical parts. Take these steps before you do anything else.

  • Set the thermostat to off, then switch off the breaker that feeds the air handler.
  • Keep people out from under any ceiling that sags, bulges or drips steadily.
  • Place a bucket under drips and move furniture and rugs out of the wet zone.
  • Do not climb into a wet attic. Insulation hides wiring and weak spots in the floor.
  • Book an HVAC technician for the drain line, then phone (817) 623-9267 about the wet ceiling.

What we do while the technician fixes the unit

An HVAC technician clears the drain line, checks the pan and restores the unit. We do not repair air conditioners. Our work starts where the water went.

A moisture meter and a thermal camera show how far the water traveled through the ceiling, wall cavities and attic floor. Wet insulation holds water like a sponge, so it is usually bagged and removed. Sagging or stained drywall is cut back to dry board. From there, air movers and LGR dehumidifiers pull moisture out of the framing and the ceiling that stays.

Where the water sat for a long stretch, we apply antimicrobial treatment to the exposed framing. If you see dark growth, our mold remediation page explains that separate job. Judging which wet materials stay follows the published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard.

Insurance and a slow drain line leak

The TDI guide treats a sudden and accidental water release and a continuous leak as two different things. A drain line that overflows one afternoon and a line that dripped for months can be treated differently. Your insurer decides, so read your policy and ask before you assume.

Take photos of the pan, the stain and the wet insulation before anything is removed. Keep the technician invoice that describes the clog. That record helps the adjuster see what happened and when.

Mistakes to avoid after an AC leak

  • Painting over the stain before the ceiling reads dry.
  • Running the system again before the drain line is cleared.
  • Pushing on a bulging ceiling to see how bad it is.
  • Skipping the attic insulation because the room looks fine.

To prevent a repeat, ask your HVAC company about the drain line and pan during routine service.
