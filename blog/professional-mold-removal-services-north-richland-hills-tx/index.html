Professional Mold Removal Services in North Richland Hills, TX

A plain list of what a proper mold removal job contains, so you can tell a real scope of work from a spray-and-go visit.

By the North Richland Hills Water Damage Pros team. Published October 26, 2026.

What a professional mold job actually includes

Professional mold removal is a controlled process with five parts. It has a written scope, a plan to keep spores out of clean rooms, removal of materials that cannot be cleaned, a fix for the moisture source, and a check that the work is finished. A visit that ends with a surface spray and no paperwork covers none of that.

Mold needs moisture to grow, so the leak, condensation or humidity problem behind it has to be found and repaired first. Cleaning an area that stays damp only resets the clock. Mold testing and remediation follow the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard, a published industry standard that crews use as a reference.

Ask for the scope in writing before any demolition starts. We explain scope and price before work starts, and you should expect the same from anyone you hire.

Containment, negative air and HEPA filtration

Containment is plastic sheeting taped around the work area, usually with a zippered door and a taped floor edge. Its job is to keep disturbed dust and spores from drifting into the rest of the house on shoes, clothing and air currents. Vents inside the work area are covered so the heating and cooling system does not carry dust to other rooms.

Negative air means a machine pulls air out of the contained area through a HEPA filter, so air leaks inward through small gaps instead of outward. The filter unit runs during demolition and cleaning. Afterward, surfaces are cleaned with a HEPA vacuum and damp wipes, because an ordinary shop vacuum can blow fine dust straight back into the room.

What comes out and what gets cleaned

Porous materials with growth, such as drywall and insulation, usually cannot be cleaned and are cut out. Solid framing and hard surfaces can often be cleaned in place. The table shows the usual approach, and your written scope should name each material the same way.

MaterialUsual approachReason
Drywall and insulation with growthCut out, bag inside containmentPorous, so growth sits below the surface
Carpet and carpet padRemove and discardFibers and foam hold moisture and debris
Wood framingHEPA vacuum, sand or brush, damp wipeSolid wood can usually be cleaned unless it is rotted
Tile, glass, sealed hard surfacesHEPA vacuum and damp wipeNon-porous, so growth stays on top
Upholstery and soft contentsJudged item by itemDepends on the material and how long it stayed damp

Debris leaves the house in sealed bags, and the crew should say where the work area begins and ends so you know which rooms to avoid.

Questions to ask before you hire

A short list of questions separates a crew with a plan from one that is improvising. Keep the answers in writing, even if that means a text or an email.

  • Will the scope list each room, each material to be removed and the containment setup?
  • Will negative air machines with HEPA filtration run during the work?
  • Who finds and repairs the moisture source, and who confirms it is dry?
  • Does the company hold the Texas license that applies to this work, and can you see it?
  • How will the job be checked at the end, and what paperwork will you receive?
  • If testing is wanted, who performs it, and is that a different party from the crew doing the cleanup?

If any answer is vague, ask again or call another company. A confident, specific answer takes a minute to give.

Post-work verification and independent testing

When removal and cleaning finish, the crew should check the area before taking down containment. That means a visual inspection, moisture readings that show the framing and surrounding materials are dry, and a smell check of the space. Photos before and after belong in your file.

Some owners also hire a separate party to take samples. That is a choice, not a requirement from us, and the results describe the air or surface on the day of sampling. Questions about how mold affects anyone in the home belong with a physician, not with a restoration company.

North Richland Hills notes: Texas licensing and city permits

Texas mold assessors and remediators are regulated under Chapter 1958 of the Occupations Code and licensed through TDLR. SB 1255 took effect September 1, 2025, and it excluded demolition after a fire or other disaster from regulated activities and repealed the provision that let an unlicensed employee do mold work under supervision. TDLR says current rules remain in effect until new rules are adopted, and it is reviewing the program.

For you, that means the license question is worth asking out loud. Ask whether the company holds the Texas license that applies to your work, and ask TDLR how the rules apply to your project, since your situation may not match a general answer.

The city runs an online E-Services permits portal for permits and inspections. Whether your repair needs a permit is a question for the city building department, so ask before demolition begins.

Selling or refinancing with mold on the record

A buyer's inspection or a lender's appraiser sometimes flags mold, and the deadline pressure that follows pushes people into rushed decisions. Ask the lender or the buyer's agent in writing what documentation they want before work begins. We cannot say what any lender or buyer will accept, and nobody honest can promise it.

Keep the written scope, photos, moisture readings, invoices and any test report in one folder. Homeowners in Hurst and Keller ask us the same questions, and the answers are identical.
