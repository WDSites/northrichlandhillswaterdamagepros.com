The first 24 to 72 hours decide how much of the house can be saved. Our 24/7 line sends a drying crew to stop the damage from spreading.

Wet carpet, a damp wall or a stain that keeps growing? That is a mitigation job

If water is on the floor or moving through a wall right now, treat it as an emergency. Our dispatch line is open 24/7. While you wait for a crew, shut off the water at the main valve. People rarely search for "water mitigation" when the floor is soaked. They search for a wet wall, a ceiling stain, carpet that will not dry or a musty smell that came back. All of those point to the same job: stopping the damage from spreading while materials can still be saved. That job is water mitigation, and it is different from the cleanup and rebuild that come after it.

What water mitigation means in the first 24 to 72 hours

Water mitigation is the work that limits damage after a leak or flood: stopping the source, removing standing water, finding where moisture traveled and drying the structure until readings return to normal. Industry practice for this work is published in ANSI/IICRC S500, and you can ask any company whether its crews work to that standard.

Drywall, insulation, carpet pad and wood framing keep absorbing and wicking water long after the puddle is gone. Moisture climbs walls, spreads under flooring and settles into cavities nobody can see. Mitigation puts instruments and airflow on the problem before swelling, staining and microbial growth set in.

When standing water is an emergency

Some situations should not wait for a morning appointment. Call the 24/7 line right away if water is still flowing, if it has reached more than one room, if the ceiling is bowing or dripping, or if you can see water near outlets, appliances or the electrical panel.

Sewage or creek water changes the plan completely. Stay out of it, do not run fans through it and call your water utility or 911 for any gas or electrical risk before you call us. We never promise an arrival time, but same-day response is available when crews are free, and after-hours calls are answered by dispatch.

How a mitigation crew works

  1. Stop the source. Close the supply line or main valve, or confirm the plumber has. Mitigation cannot finish while water is still entering.
  2. Map the moisture. Moisture meters read wood, drywall and subfloor. A thermal camera shows cold, wet patches behind finishes so the crew knows where to open up.
  3. Extract. Truck-mounted or portable extraction units pull standing water and water held in carpet. Wet pad is usually removed instead of dried.
  4. Set the drying system. Air movers push air across wet surfaces and low grain refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the air so drying keeps going.
  5. Monitor daily. The crew returns, repeats readings and adjusts equipment until each material reads dry. Then the equipment comes out.

Ask for the readings in writing. They show your insurer how the loss was measured and when drying was finished.

Structural drying: where most of the time goes

Structural drying is the part homeowners underestimate. Water that soaks into the bottom plate of a wall, a hardwood subfloor or the cavity above a ceiling does not leave by itself. Crews often drill small holes, lift baseboards or cut a short flood cut in drywall so air can reach wet cavities.

Drying takes days, and the calendar is not the measure. Readings are. A floor that feels dry to the hand can still hold enough moisture to cup boards or feed mold, which is why a crew checks the same spots day after day instead of leaving equipment for a fixed number of hours.

If you hear a company promise an exact finish time before they have taken a single reading, ask what the promise is based on.

What drives the cost of mitigation

We do not quote from a photo, and no honest company can. A real number depends on a handful of facts that only an inspection reveals.

  • The water category: clean supply water, gray water or contaminated water changes the labor and what must be removed.
  • How many square feet and how many rooms are wet, including the cavities behind finishes.
  • The materials involved, since hardwood, plaster and engineered flooring dry differently.
  • How many days of drying and how much equipment the readings call for.
  • How much demolition and rebuild the drying leaves behind.

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered by homeowners insurance, while gradual leaks and neglect often are not, and flood damage usually needs a separate flood policy.
